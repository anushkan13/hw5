Reflection

Looking back at the canonical run — three tickets, $3,400.00 in, $152.00 out.

Q1. How did the agents perform on each ticket, and why?

Ticket 101 went well. It took 4 runs and closed resolved. The Boss called Inventory first, which was right, because the whole question was whether we had the white tee in small. Inventory ran check_stock and got 0 for CC-TEE-WHITE/S, so it handed off to Accounting, which found invoice 501 ($840, due 2026-08-28, already overdue) sitting in front of Bulldog Print Co, our restock vendor on a 5 day lead. Accounting also pulled get_margin even though nothing on 101 needed margin, so a little extra, but harmless. The team paid the overdue invoice plus a small $8 tee restock, and Customer Service drafted a reply to Tauhid Zaman. Facilities never got pulled in, which is correct since nothing about 101 was a building problem. The one honest flaw: stock stayed at 0 the whole time because our date is frozen at 2026-08-31 and the restock can't physically arrive, so the team couldn't close it on its own. It needed a human instruction to call it resolved.

Ticket 102 was the cleanest run. 3 runs, resolved, and the Boss went straight to Facilities and nowhere else. Facilities did the cash check and the payment prep itself, so Accounting never touched this one, which is actually one hop shorter than we planned and totally fine. Best moment of the whole homework: the Boss caught a bookkeeping conflict (request #3 was marked approved_and_paid but the details said awaiting approval) and refused to move until it was reconciled. That is exactly the kind of thing you want an agent to stop on instead of blindly paying $2,400 of rent twice.

Ticket 103 was the messiest, which makes sense because it was the hardest. 2 runs, and the second run hit 79 steps, the biggest single run of the three tickets. The first run actually stalled waiting for a target price before it could do anything useful, which was fair since the whole ticket was a pricing question. Once it had the numbers ($50/hoodie, $1,000 for 20, $28 margin a unit) it resolved the pricing cleanly. Where it hit a wall was the restock: check_stock showed 8 of 20 in stock, short 12, and the 12 unit restock came to $264, but we only had $152 left. So request #4 stayed awaiting_approval, never got approved, and those 12 units are flagged "not secured." That's not the agent failing, that's the agent correctly refusing to spend money we don't have. Customer Service also drafted two replies to the Yale AI Club here, and at one point delegated back up to the Boss, the only reverse delegation in all three tickets, which is nice because it proves the team really is fully connected both directions.

Q2. How did the Actual runs compare to the Expected plan on each ticket?

101 matched the plan closely. We expected Boss to Inventory to Accounting, and that's what happened. The two surprises were small: Accounting read get_margin when we hadn't expected margin to matter on 101, and the ticket couldn't self close because of the frozen date, so a human had to tell it to resolve. Neither broke the plan.

102 came in shorter than planned. We expected Facilities to loop in Accounting for the money side, but Facilities handled the cash check and payment prep on its own and Accounting never got involved. So one fewer hop than Expected, and the result was still correct. The bookkeeping conflict catch wasn't something we scripted either, it just came out of the agent doing its job.

103 was the biggest gap between Expected and Actual, but mostly in effort, not direction. We expected a pricing analysis plus a restock, and that's what it attempted. What we didn't predict was the 79 step second run and the first run stalling for a price. We also assumed the invoice 501 block might show up again, but it didn't, because 101 had already paid 501 off, so by the time 103 ran, 501's blocks_shipping was false. And the restock we planned for simply couldn't complete because the cash wasn't there ($152 against a $264 need, short $112).

Across all three: cash reconciled exactly. Start $3,400, minus $848 on 101, minus $2,400 on 102, $0 on 103, ending at $152, which matches cash_accounts. Three payments, all approved by the Shop Owner, and no agent called make_payment itself on any of these runs, which was the whole point of the approval gate.

Q3. What would have been simpler as a single agent with tools, and why?

Ticket 102 is the clearest case. It was really one job: check we had the cash, then pay the $2,400 rent. The Boss ended up delegating to Facilities, and Facilities then did the cash check and payment prep by itself anyway, so the "team" was basically one agent wearing a Boss hat for a second. A single agent with check_cash, a payment prep tool, and the approval step would have done the exact same work with none of the handoff overhead, and it still would have caught the bookkeeping conflict because that came from reading the data, not from having five agents.

You can see the cost of the multi agent setup in two places. On 103, Customer Service drafted two separate replies to the Yale AI Club, which is the kind of duplication you get when work is spread across agents instead of one agent tracking what it already said. And we had to give the Boss a bigger budget (28 model requests and 40 tool calls) than the specialists (14 and 20) just to keep the coordination from running out, which is overhead that a single tool using agent wouldn't need at all. For straightforward money errands like 102, one agent with tools is simpler and cheaper.

Q4. Three new problems Campus Customs could hit that THIS team could already solve with the tools we built:

  1. A vendor invoice comes due and threatens to block a restock. We literally did this on 101 with invoice 501. Accounting can pull the invoice, check it against cash, and the team can route it through the human approval gate and pay it, same path we already ran.
  2. Rent or a recurring bill needs paying and we need to confirm we can afford it first. This is 102 almost exactly. Facilities or Accounting runs check_cash, preps the payment, and the Shop Owner approves it, with the no negative balance guard making sure we don't overdraw.
  3. A customer asks whether an item is in stock and when they can get it. Inventory runs check_stock, Accounting can check the restock lead time and cost, and Customer Service drafts the reply, which is the 101 and 103 pattern combined. The team can already price it, check availability, and write the customer back.

Q5. Three new problems it could NOT solve, and what tools or agents we'd need to add:

  1. Actually receiving a restock shipment. Right now the white tee on 101 can never be fulfilled, because our date is frozen at 2026-08-31 and nothing we order can physically arrive. We'd need a receive_shipment tool that marks stock as received, plus some way to advance the clock, so a restock actually lands instead of leaving stock at 0 forever.
  2. Knowing whether the shop is actually making money. We have get_margin per item, but there's no record of sales, so the team can't answer "are we profitable this month" or "can we afford the hoodie restock out of recent revenue." On 103 the restock died because we only had $152 and no incoming cash. We'd need a sales table and a Finance agent that reads revenue over time, not just the current cash drawer.
  3. Comparing vendor quotes before we buy. On 103 we were short $112 on 12 hoodie units at $22 each, and we had no way to see if another vendor could do it cheaper. We only ever look at Bulldog Print Co. We'd need a Procurement agent plus a get_quotes tool that pulls prices from multiple vendors so the team can pick the cheapest option instead of taking the one lead time and price we happen to have.
